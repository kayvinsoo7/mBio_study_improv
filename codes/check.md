# First, Do No Harm: Responsible Computing for Climate-Conscious Bioinformatics and Healthcare Research

## Short Abstract
Bioinformatics and healthcare research increasingly rely on intensive computational workflows, posing significant environmental impacts. This paper highlights the necessity of integrating environmental considerations into bioinformatics practices by quantifying the energy and carbon footprints of standard pipelines (e.g., GWAS, PRS, phylogenetics, metagenomics). We propose a novel responsible computing framework and policy integration strategy—Environmental Accountability in Research Systems for Africa (EARS-Africa)—to establish sustainable research practices, including computational-to-environmental feedback loops, institutional energy quotas, and incentive-driven efficiency. By demonstrating hypothetical energy optimizations and CO₂ savings through synthetic case studies, we advocate for responsible computational practices in bioinformatics to mitigate climate impacts.

## Introduction
Computational research in bioinformatics and healthcare is critical for scientific discovery, yet it carries substantial and often overlooked environmental costs. The intensive use of computational resources has expanded exponentially, mirroring advancements in machine learning, data mining, natural language processing, and robotics applications. Studies such as Grealey's (2023) quantification of bioinformatics' carbon footprint illustrate the escalating climate impact, underscoring an urgent need for sustainable computational methodologies.

This abstract argues for explicit consideration of computational environmental impacts, supported by quantitative metrics of energy usage and carbon footprints, and proposes actionable strategies for institutional policy adaptation.

## Methods – Proposed Framework
We introduce a Responsible Computing Framework tailored for bioinformatics and healthcare research, structured as follows:

### Step 1: Quantitative Energy and Carbon Accounting
- Quantify baseline energy consumption (kWh) and carbon emissions (kg CO₂) of commonly executed bioinformatics tasks (GWAS, PRS, etc.).
- Measure pipeline inefficiencies (repeated or redundant computational tasks) and identify optimization opportunities.

### Step 2: Computational-to-Environmental Feedback Loop
- Develop a visualized feedback loop illustrating the computational demand, resultant carbon emissions, and potential energy savings achievable through workflow optimization and efficient coding practices.

### Step 3: Institutional Integration – EARS-Africa
Beyond technological recommendations, the EARS-Africa framework is proposed as an institutional policy integrating:
- **Energy quotas**: Allocating energy budgets per laboratory or research project.
- **Incentives**: Rewarding researchers who adopt energy-efficient computational workflows and optimized code.
- **Green Review Boards**: Establishing committees that review computational plans within research grant applications, prioritizing environmentally responsible practices.
